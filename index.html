Case Context

Copper Cup is evaluating a limited direct cooperative pilot called The Story of the Bean. The draft plan would pay a premium, fund a basic traceability page, and introduce a featured latte at a planned price of $5.45. Copper Cup would describe the proposed relationship and the evidence it can verify without claiming that one purchase solves every social or environmental problem in coffee.

Three months later, just as the launch menu is being finalized, Elena receives two messages.

Message 1: Cooperative Update

Heavy rain during harvest and drying reduced the amount of coffee that met the quality target. The cooperative can still supply the pilot, but fewer bags are available and the quoted price is higher.

Message 2: Customer Interest

A local food creator posts about the hidden journey behind everyday products. Copper Cup's customer questions and advance interest in traceable coffee increase almost overnight.

Elena looks at the two messages and asks Maya, “How can the same coffee become harder to get at exactly the moment more people want it?”

Maya recognizes that Copper Cup needs an economics analysis before choosing its pricing response.

Economics decision: How should Copper Cup respond when supply tightens, demand rises, and both the business and its customers face scarcity?

Economics: When the Bean Becomes a Price

Maya begins with the definition from her Economics module: economics is the study of choice in the presence of scarcity. Scarcity does not mean that coffee has disappeared. It means Copper Cup cannot have unlimited high-quality beans, unlimited roasting time, unlimited cash, unlimited employee attention, and unlimited customer spending power.

The cooperative has fewer qualifying beans. Copper Cup has only so much cash available before launch. The roaster has limited capacity. Students near campus have limited budgets. Every possible response uses a scarce resource and gives something else up.

Elena could keep the drink price low, but Copper Cup would absorb more cost and have less cash for other operations. She could raise the price, but some students might buy less often or choose another drink. She could make the roast a limited release, but fewer customers would experience the product. She could blend the coffee with another origin, but the relationship between the product and the cooperative would become less clear.

Those are opportunity costs. The opportunity cost of a choice is the next-best alternative given up. It may not appear on an invoice. Keeping the price unchanged has an accounting cost because Copper Cup earns less margin, but it also has opportunity costs: money used to absorb the higher bean cost cannot be used for employee hours, equipment maintenance, or another product.

Before drawing the curves, Maya defines one market clearly: Copper Cup's launch-week market for the Story of the Bean latte. Price means the menu price per latte, and quantity means the number of featured lattes customers buy per day. This keeps the upstream market for green coffee separate from Copper Cup's downstream market for a prepared drink.

Heavy rain reduces the cooperative's qualifying harvest and raises Copper Cup's quoted bean cost. At each possible latte price, Copper Cup is now willing and able to offer fewer featured lattes per day, so the retail supply curve shifts left. The local creator's post increases the number of featured lattes customers want at each possible price, so retail demand shifts right. Both changes create upward pressure on the latte's equilibrium price.

The effect on quantity is less certain. A supply decrease tends to reduce equilibrium quantity. A demand increase tends to raise it. Without knowing the relative size of the shifts, Maya cannot honestly claim that the final quantity will definitely rise or definitely fall.

At the original planned price of $5.45, the new market conditions may create a shortage: customers want more Story of the Bean lattes than Copper Cup can profitably provide. That shortage creates upward price pressure. A higher price gives customers a reason to reconsider how much they buy and gives Copper Cup an incentive to offer more when possible. But adjustment takes time. A new coffee harvest cannot appear simply because today's menu price increased.

The model is useful because it strips the situation down to a few relationships. It is also limited. It does not decide what Copper Cup should value, whether a price is fair, or who should bear the cost of climate and market risk. Those questions require evidence and judgment.

Maya notices another complication. A market price can coordinate buyers and sellers, but it may not include every cost. If soil damage, carbon emissions, unstable farmer income, or community impacts fall on people who are not part of the transaction, economists call those third-party effects externalities. Markets can also work differently when buyers have market power, when information is incomplete, or when public policy changes incentives.

Economics therefore gives Copper Cup more than a reason to raise prices. It gives the company a disciplined way to identify tradeoffs, predict likely responses, examine assumptions, and ask who benefits, who pays, and what the market price leaves out.

Economics in Action

Maya learns that economists do not study only prices. They use models, data, forecasting, and careful communication to understand choices made by households, businesses, and governments.

  • Prices, Inflation, and Labor: Bart Hobijn's work connects price setting, inflation, labor markets, and macroeconomic data. Copper Cup should distinguish a product-specific coffee shock from broader inflation affecting wages, rent, shipping, and student budgets.
  • Incentives and Work: Alexander Bick's research examines how policies and incentives affect decisions about work. Copper Cup can ask how wages, schedules, taxes, and the cost of living influence both employees and customers.
  • Climate and Policy: Stephie Fried studies how climate change and expected climate policies influence decisions today. The cooperative and Copper Cup may respond before a policy arrives because future weather, regulation, and costs already shape risk.
  • Trade and Development: Wyatt Brooks connects international trade with economic development. Coffee moves through global trade, but the benefits and risks of that trade may be distributed differently across producers, intermediaries, businesses, and consumers.
  • Externalities and Social Costs: Alejandro Manelli and Kelly Bishop emphasize third-party effects and environmental questions. A market price may omit costs borne by communities, workers, or the environment.
  • Human Capital and Opportunity: Esteban Aucejo studies education, skills, and labor-market outcomes. Maya sees that economics can connect individual investments in knowledge with later choices, productivity, wages, and opportunity.

Economics Decision Point

Copper Cup must set the launch price before the final order is placed. Each option changes incentives, access, and who absorbs the higher cost.

Planning note: These are decision estimates, not guaranteed outcomes. Maya will compare them with actual sales, stockouts, and customer responses during the pilot.

Option A: Raise the Featured Price

Offer: Raise the latte from $5.45 to $5.95 and offer up to 60 per day.

Business upside: Restores the estimated $2.85 contribution margin per latte.

Business risk: Customer response at $5.95 is unknown, and some customers may buy less or feel excluded.

Social question: Who is left out when responsibility is built into a premium price?

Option B: Keep the Price Unchanged

Offer: Keep the latte at $5.45 and offer up to 60 per day.

Business upside: Protects affordability and customer goodwill.

Business risk: Margin falls to an estimated $2.35 per latte, and demand may exceed the daily supply.

Social question: If customers do not pay the added cost, who does?

Option C: Use Tiered Access

Offer: Allocate the limited beans to as many as 45 lattes at $5.95 and 15 small drip coffees at $3.25 per day.

Business upside: Preserves a lower-price way to experience the cooperative coffee.

Business risk: Adds batch, inventory, and communication complexity while reducing margin on the lower-price servings.

Social question: Can Copper Cup widen access without hiding the product's true cost?

AI at Work: Comparing Price Options

Maya could ask AI to compare expected margin, likely customer response, and stakeholder effects. A useful comparison must state assumptions about price sensitivity, available quantity, fixed and variable costs, and customer substitutes. The final choice remains a human business judgment.
